# {{ page.title }}

NLP workshops have a rich tradition, with topics ranging from representation learning to text technologies for social sciences and speech communities, as well as meta-areas such as teaching NLP and CL. Some workshops span decades and can metamorphose into specialized conferences (e.g., the conference of machine translation -WMT-, where the “W” indicates its provenance as a Workshop).

While my impression is that workshop papers tend to be perceived as of slightly lower value when compared with conference papers, skimming the smaller workshop volumes can be quite entertaining. Also you don’t have to crawl through a mountain of conference proceedings papers - simply find a workshop with a topic that interests you, and then explore the papers! (Since the workshop landscape is quite dynamic, individual venues are best inspected on a per-year basis in the ACL anthology, e.g., see [this link](https://aclanthology.org/events/ws-2026/) for all ACL-related workshops of 2026.)

Expectations towards meeting conventions may be slightly lower for workshop papers, but doesn't need to be a downside. Instead, it makes those papers often a bit less dense as they cut the authors a bit of slack, which can help allow for presentation of more curious ideas. (As long as AI reviewers stay out 🙂!)

## Three Recent Workshop Papers that Stuck with Me

- [On Tables with Numbers, with Numbers](https://aclanthology.org/2025.lm4uc-1.12/) (Kogkalidis & Chatzikyriakidis, LM4UC 2025). This paper looks at the obsession with numbers in empirical sciences and argues that it goes too far. The issue is that in the fog of numbers, we may forget what they stand for, making us overlook if the measurements that the numbers represent are actually what we want to measure. The linked paper includes an extremely provocative claim: “Tables are irrelevant” - but it makes some strong points, for instance the infatuation with numbers may privilege high-resource labs that can run many experiments, including many redundant ones. Good food for thought!

- [Degree Zero of Translation: Using Interlinear Baselines to Quantify Translator Intervention](https://aclanthology.org/2026.latechclfl-1.22/) (Rapacz & Smywiński-Pohl, LaTeCH-CLfL 2026). From this paper I take it that the time has come where we should focus less on whether a translation is correct, but also take other features into account. The paper proposes a radical metric of translator intervention. The metric is qualitatively neutral, by which I mean non-judgemental: a translation can have a large amount of intervention and be considered highly useful for some audience, and it can have a minimal amount of intervention and be considered preferable by another audience. Gave me a push to think about translation quality measurements in a new light.

- [Superframes: A Schema for Lexicon-free Frame-semantic Annotation](https://aclanthology.org/2026.dmr-1.11/) (Evang, DMR 2026). What is the difference between “owning something” and “buying something”? A key difference is that owning implies a state of possession, whereas buying initializes a possession. Since annotating text with such linguistic structures is cumbersome and usually requires extensive and complicated resources, “Superframes” proposes an innovative, fairly lightweight and multilingual semantic annotation scheme, building on the beloved syntactic [Universal Dependency Schema](https://universaldependencies.org/). This could be very useful for structured linguistic analyses and searches of corpora, including multi-lingual ones!

## Conclusion

There are many gems to be found in workshop proceedings! The three papers above is just a small, non-representative sample - I'm sure that I've overlooked many similarly interesting papers!

So the take-away from this post is: Do not view Workshops as a publication-venue of lesser value. View them as a rich, diverse mine of ideas and specialized studies.
